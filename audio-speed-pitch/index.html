What is Audio Speed and Pitch Changer?

Audio Speed and Pitch Changer lets you change how fast a recording plays and how high or low it sounds, separately. Slow down a song to learn it, speed up a lecture to save time, or move music into a key that suits your voice.

Speed goes from 0.25× to 4× and pitch from −12 to +12 semitones (one octave). Tick the record player option to change both together, for nightcore or slowed versions.

The tool uses time-stretching (WSOLA) and high-quality resampling that run in your browser, with a 15-second preview before you save as MP3 or WAV.

What you can use it for

Learning songs

Slow a song down to 0.75× to hear fast parts clearly while it stays in tune.

Singing in your key

Move a backing track up or down a few semitones.

Lectures and podcasts

Speed up speech to 1.25× or 1.5× without the cartoon voice.

Nightcore and slowed edits

Change speed and pitch together like a record player.

Dance and fitness

Match the tempo of music to a routine.

Language practice

Slow down audio to follow every word.

Step-by-step tutorial: how to use Audio Speed and Pitch Changer

  1. Open a file

    Click the box or drop an MP3, M4A, WAV, OGG or FLAC file. Videos work too; their sound is used.

  2. Set the speed

    Drag the Speed slider, press a preset such as 0.75× or 1.25×, or type an exact value. The new length is shown below.

  3. Set the pitch

    Drag the Pitch slider, press +1, −2 and so on, or type a value with decimals for fine tuning.

  4. Or link them

    Tick "Change the pitch together with the speed" for the record player effect. The pitch then follows the speed automatically.

  5. Preview

    Type where the preview should start and press "Preview 15 seconds".

  6. Save

    Choose MP3 or WAV and press "Change and save". When it is done, listen in the player and press Download.

Worked example: practising a fast guitar solo

Lina wants to learn a 4-minute song. She opens it, presses 0.75× (the new length becomes 5:20) and leaves the pitch at 0 so she can play along in tune. She sets "Preview from" to 2:10, where the solo starts, and listens. Then she moves the pitch to −1 because her guitar is tuned half a step down, saves an MP3 and downloads song-0.75x-down1.mp3.

Tips and tricks

  • For practice, slow down in small steps: 0.9×, then 0.8×, then 0.75×.
  • Moving a song by 1 to 3 semitones sounds most natural.
  • For speech, 1.25× to 1.5× stays easy to follow.
  • Use WAV if you will edit the result again, to avoid quality loss.
  • Preview the hardest part of the song before saving a long file.

Problems and how to fix them

It sounds echoey or grainy

Large changes are hard for any time-stretching method. Try a smaller change, or use the record player mode which avoids stretching.

The file could not be read

Your browser cannot decode this format. Convert it to MP3 with the Audio Converter first.

It ran out of memory

Very long files (over an hour) need a lot of memory. Cut the file into parts with the Audio Cutter.

The pitch slider is greyed out

The record player option is ticked, so the pitch follows the speed. Untick it to set the pitch yourself.

Questions and answers

Can I slow down a song without making it sound deeper?

Yes. Speed and pitch are separate: change only the speed and the song keeps its key; change only the pitch and it keeps its tempo.

What is a semitone?

The smallest step in Western music, one piano key. 12 semitones make an octave. +2 moves a song up one whole tone, which is common for singing in a different key.

What does the record player option do?

It changes speed and pitch together, like playing a record faster or slower. This is how "nightcore" (faster and higher) and "slowed" (slower and deeper) versions are made.

Is my file uploaded?

No. All processing happens on your device.

Does it lower the quality?

Small changes sound natural. Very big changes (below 0.5× or above 2×, or more than about 5 semitones) can sound a bit grainy or echoey, as with any time-stretching tool.

How long does it take?

Roughly a few seconds per minute of stereo audio on a computer; longer on a phone.
