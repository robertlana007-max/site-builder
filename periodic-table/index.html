What is Periodic Table?

The Periodic Table shows all 118 chemical elements in their groups and periods, coloured by category, with the lanthanides and actinides in their own rows. Click any element to see its atomic number, atomic weight, category, group, period, block, electron configuration, state at room temperature and electronegativity.

It also includes a molar mass calculator: type a formula like H2O, Ca(OH)2 or CuSO4·5H2O and get the molar mass with a breakdown of every element.

It works offline in your browser and fits phones with a list view and a scrollable table.

What you can use it for

Chemistry homework

Look up atomic masses and electron configurations quickly.

Molar mass and moles

Work out the molar mass of a compound for mole and stoichiometry calculations.

Learning the table

See where metals, nonmetals, metalloids and noble gases sit, and filter by category.

Mass percentages

Find what percentage of a compound's mass comes from each element.

Quick reference

Search by name, symbol or number on any device.

Step-by-step tutorial: how to use Periodic Table

  1. Find an element

    Click it in the table, or type its name, symbol or atomic number in the search box. Pressing Enter selects the first match.

  2. Read its details

    The panel below the table shows the atomic weight, category, group, period, block, electron configuration, state at 20 °C and Pauling electronegativity.

  3. Filter by category

    Click a coloured category, such as Halogen, to fade out all other elements. Click it again to show them all.

  4. Use the list view on phones

    Press List to see every element as a row, which is easier on a small screen. The table view can also be scrolled sideways inside its box.

  5. Calculate a molar mass

    Type a formula in the molar mass box, such as Al2(SO4)3. Brackets, square brackets and hydrate dots (· or .) are supported, and a number in front multiplies a part, like 5H2O.

  6. Read the breakdown

    The table lists each element, the number of atoms, its atomic mass, its share of the total in g/mol and its mass percentage.

Features

  • All 118 elementsWith IUPAC standard atomic weights, or the mass number of the most stable isotope in brackets for radioactive elements.
  • Category coloursEleven categories with a clickable legend.
  • Electron configurationsNoble-gas shorthand including the real exceptions such as Cr, Cu, Pd and Au.
  • State and electronegativityState at room temperature and Pauling electronegativity where known.
  • Molar mass calculatorHandles nested brackets, hydrates and coefficients, with mass percentages.
  • Phone friendlyA list view and a table that scrolls inside its own box.

Worked example: copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate

Type CuSO4·5H2O. The calculator counts 1 Cu, 1 S, 9 O and 10 H atoms. Using Cu 63.546, S 32.06, O 15.999 and H 1.008: 63.546 + 32.06 + 9 × 15.999 + 10 × 1.008 = 63.546 + 32.06 + 143.991 + 10.08 = 249.677 g/mol. The breakdown shows copper is about 25.45% of the mass and the water about 36%.

Tips and tricks

  • Element symbols start with a capital letter: Co is cobalt, but CO is carbon monoxide.
  • Use brackets for groups that repeat: Ca(OH)2 has two O and two H.
  • Atomic weights in [brackets] belong to elements with no stable isotopes and are mass numbers, not averages.
  • Electronegativity rises across a period and falls down a group; fluorine is the highest at 3.98.
  • Configurations for elements above 103 are predicted, as only a few atoms have ever been made.

Problems and how to fix them

It says a symbol is not an element

Check the capital letters. "CL" is read as C and L, so type Cl for chlorine.

A bracket error appears

Every "(" or "[" needs a matching closing bracket.

The table is too wide on my phone

Swipe sideways inside the table box, or press List for a one-column view.

My molar mass differs slightly from my textbook

Books may use older or more rounded atomic weights, such as 1.0079 for hydrogen. The difference is usually in the second decimal place.

Questions and answers

Which atomic weights are used?

IUPAC standard atomic weights (abridged). Radioactive elements with no stable isotope show the mass number of their most stable isotope in brackets.

Which formulas can the molar mass calculator read?

Formulas with brackets, square brackets, hydrate dots and coefficients, such as Ca(OH)2, K4[Fe(CN)6] and CuSO4·5H2O.

Are the electron configurations correct for exceptions?

Yes. Known exceptions such as chromium, copper, palladium, gold and several actinides are shown correctly. Configurations from element 104 on are predicted.

How do I use it on a phone?

Swipe the table sideways inside its box, or switch to List view.

Does it work offline?

Yes. All data is built into the page.
