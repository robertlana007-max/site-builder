What is Colour Blindness Simulator?

The Colour Blindness Simulator shows how an image looks to people with different types of colour vision deficiency. Drop in a photo, a chart, a map, a website screenshot or a design, and see it as someone with red-blindness, green-blindness, blue-blindness or no colour vision would see it.

It uses the widely accepted Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) model, which also covers milder forms (protanomaly, deuteranomaly and tritanomaly) with a strength slider. You can view the result side by side, with a drag slider, or as a grid of all types, and download the simulated image or a labelled comparison.

Around 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of colour blindness. Designers, teachers, developers and data analysts use simulators like this to check that charts, maps, signs and interfaces still make sense without relying on colour alone.

What you can use it for

Check charts and dashboards

Make sure the lines and bars in your chart can still be told apart, or add labels and patterns where they cannot.

Test website and app designs

Check that buttons, error messages and links do not depend only on red or green.

Teaching and awareness

Show students or colleagues what colour blindness is actually like.

Maps, signs and posters

Make sure transport maps, warning signs and posters work for everyone.

Game and product design

Check that colour-coded items, pieces or cables can still be identified.

Step-by-step tutorial: how to use Colour Blindness Simulator

  1. Add an image

    Click the box, drop an image onto it or paste a screenshot with Ctrl+V. You can also press "Try with a sample picture" to see a colourful test chart.

  2. Pick a type

    In "Type of colour blindness" choose Red-blind (protan), Green-blind (deutan), Blue-blind (tritan) or No colour (achromatopsia). Green-blind is selected first because it is the most common.

  3. Set the strength

    At 100% (full) you see protanopia, deuteranopia or tritanopia: one type of cone is missing. Lower values show the milder and more common forms: protanomaly, deuteranomaly or tritanomaly.

  4. Choose a view

    "Side by side" shows normal vision next to the simulation. "Slider" lets you drag a divider across one image. "All types" shows every type at once in a grid.

  5. Read the note

    Under the pictures you see a short description of the chosen type and how common it is.

  6. Download

    "Download simulated image" saves the full-size simulation. "Download comparison" saves a labelled side-by-side or grid image, as PNG or JPG.

Features

  • Accepted scienceMachado et al. (2009) matrices applied in linear RGB, the same model used by many accessibility tools.
  • All main typesProtanopia, deuteranopia, tritanopia and achromatopsia, plus milder anomalous forms from 10% to 100%.
  • Three viewsSide by side, an interactive drag slider, and a grid of all types.
  • Full-size downloadsSimulate the whole image at its original size, or save a labelled comparison.
  • Paste screenshotsPress Ctrl+V to check a screenshot of a web page or app straight away.
  • PrivateImages are processed in your browser and never uploaded.

Worked example: checking a sales chart

Sam made a bar chart where profit is green and loss is red, and pastes a screenshot of it with Ctrl+V. In Side by side with Green-blind at 100%, both bar colours turn into almost the same mustard yellow. He switches to All types and sees the same problem for Red-blind. He changes the loss bars to orange-red with a striped pattern and adds value labels, checks again and both kinds of bars are now clearly different in every view.

Tips and tricks

  • Never use colour as the only way to show meaning. Add labels, icons, patterns or different line styles.
  • Red and green, green and brown, and blue and purple are the pairs most often confused.
  • Check both strong and mild forms: many people have the milder deuteranomaly rather than full deuteranopia.
  • Use the All types view and download the grid to share an accessibility check with your team.
  • Simulations are an approximation. Real experiences vary from person to person, so ask colour-blind users for feedback when you can.

Problems and how to fix them

The simulated image looks almost the same

That is good news: the colours in your image are not ones that this type of colour blindness confuses. Try another type or the All types view.

The download is very large

PNG keeps every pixel exactly. Choose JPG next to the download buttons for a much smaller file.

The image will not open

Use a JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF or BMP file. HEIC photos from iPhones need converting with the HEIC to JPG tool first.

The page is slow with huge images

The preview uses a smaller copy, but the full-size download is processed in your browser. Very large images (over 50 megapixels) are refused; resize them first.

Questions and answers

How accurate is the simulation?

It uses the Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) model, which is widely used in research and accessibility tools. It is a good approximation, but each person's colour vision is a little different.

What do the names mean?

Protan is red-blind, deutan is green-blind and tritan is blue-blind. At 100% (the -opia forms) one type of cone is missing; lower strengths show the milder, more common -anomaly forms. Achromatopsia means seeing no colour.

Which type is the most common?

Deuteranomaly, a mild green weakness, affects about 5 in 100 men. In total around 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some colour vision deficiency.

Is my image uploaded?

No. The simulation runs in your browser.

How do I make my design colour-blind friendly?

Do not rely on colour alone: add labels, icons, patterns or different line styles, and avoid red against green. Then check again here.
