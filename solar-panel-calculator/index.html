What is Solar Panel Calculator?

The Solar Panel Calculator gives a first estimate of the solar system you need: the number of panels and total kW, yearly production, the battery bank in kWh and Ah, the inverter size and the charge controller current. It works anywhere in the world because you enter your own sun hours.

Start from your monthly bill, your daily kWh, or a list of your appliances with watts and hours. A rough guide fills in typical sun hours for different climates, and you can add your electricity price to estimate savings.

What you can use it for

Plan a home system

Know the size before asking installers for quotes.

Off-grid cabins

Size panels and batteries for a cabin or farm.

Backup power

Plan batteries for power cuts.

See whether an offer is in the right range.

Learn the basics

Understand how sun hours, losses and batteries fit together.

Step-by-step tutorial: how to use Solar Panel Calculator

  1. Enter your use

    Choose your monthly kWh from a bill, your daily kWh, or list your appliances with watts, number and hours a day.

  2. Enter sun hours

    Type your peak sun hours, or choose a rough guide for your climate.

  3. Set panels and losses

    Type your panel size in watts and the system losses (20% is typical).

  4. Choose coverage

    Set what share of your use the panels should cover.

  5. Set batteries

    Set the share of daily use to come from batteries, days of backup, battery type and voltage. Use 0% for a system without batteries.

  6. Read your system

    See panels, array size, roof space, yearly production, battery, inverter and charge controller sizes, and optional savings.

Worked example: a 400 kWh per month home

400 kWh a month is about 13.15 kWh a day. With 4.5 sun hours and 20% losses, the panels must make 13.15 ÷ (4.5 × 0.8) = 3.65 kW, so nine 450 W panels (4.05 kW), producing about 5,322 kWh a year. Covering half a day's use from lithium batteries needs about 7.3 kWh of storage, and a 3,000 W peak load needs a 4 kW inverter.

Tips and tricks

  • For off-grid systems, use the sun hours of your darkest month.
  • Shade on even one panel can cut a whole string's output.
  • 48 V batteries are best for most homes.
  • Cut your use first: efficient appliances make the system smaller and cheaper.

Problems and how to fix them

Peak sun hours out of range

Use a value between about 2 and 7; look up your location on a solar map if unsure.

The charge current is very high

Choose a higher battery voltage or plan for several controllers.

Savings seem low

Savings only count solar energy that replaces bought electricity; export payments are not included.

Questions and answers

What are peak sun hours?

The day's sunlight counted as hours of full-strength sun (1,000 W per m²). Most places get 2.5–6.5 on a yearly average.

Why add system losses?

Heat, dust, wiring, the inverter and battery all waste some energy. 15–25% is typical.

Do I need batteries?

Only if you want power at night or during outages. For a grid-tied system without backup, set the battery share to 0.

Which battery voltage should I choose?

12 V suits very small systems. 24 V or 48 V is better for homes because the current and cable sizes are lower.

Is this enough to buy a system?

It is a first estimate. Get a site survey from a qualified installer who checks shade, roof direction and local rules.
